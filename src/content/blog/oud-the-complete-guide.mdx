No note in perfumery asks more of you than oud. It rewards patience, resists easy categorisation, and has a way of smelling completely different on Tuesday than it did on Saturday. Collectors either fall into it completely or find it impenetrable. Very few are indifferent.

Understanding oud starts not with fragrance, but with a tree.

## What oud actually is

Aquilaria is a genus of trees native to Southeast and South Asia. Under normal conditions, the wood is pale, odourless, unremarkable. What transforms it into one of the most prized raw materials in perfumery is disease.

When an Aquilaria tree is infected by a specific mould — _Phialophora parasitica_ — it responds by producing a dense, dark resin within its heartwood. This is the tree's defence mechanism. Over years, sometimes decades, the resin saturates the wood, turning it from pale and light to dark, heavy, and extraordinarily fragrant. The infected wood is called agarwood. The oil extracted from it is oud.

The rarity is real. Only a fraction of wild Aquilaria trees ever develop the infection. The trees that do are increasingly scarce — overharvesting has pushed several Aquilaria species toward endangerment. Genuine wild oud commands extraordinary prices, sometimes more per gram than gold.

What makes it worth that price is a scent profile unlike anything else in the natural world: simultaneously animalic and woody, medicinal and sweet, ancient and alive.

## Why origin changes everything

Oud from a Hindi tree smells categorically different from oud from a Cambodian tree. Both are technically the same material — agarwood resin — but origin, climate, soil, and the specific strain of infecting mould produce wildly different aromatic profiles.

**Hindi oud** (from India and Bangladesh) is the most polarising. It leans animalic and barnyard, with a richness that reads as almost fecal to some noses and profoundly sensual to others. It is the oud that non-oud wearers find most confronting. It is also, to many serious collectors, the most compelling.

**Cambodian oud** is generally sweeter and more approachable. There's a honeyed quality, sometimes a light fruitiness, with the woody resinous character sitting more prominently than the animalic. It tends to be the entry point for Western collectors encountering natural oud for the first time.

**Thai oud** sits between Hindi and Cambodian in some respects — woody and resinous, with a distinct mentholated or cooling edge that gives it a clarity the others don't have. It's cleaner in feel without being thin.

**Laotian oud** is often described as the most refined. Deep, woody, with a subtle sweetness and none of the confrontational animalic character of Hindi. It's the oud for people who think they don't like oud.

These are generalisations — within each region, quality and character vary enormously by specific location, tree age, and harvesting method. But knowing these broad profiles gives you a framework for understanding why two ouds that are ostensibly the same material can smell like completely different substances.

## The four faces of oud

Whatever the origin, oud expresses itself across four primary dimensions. Most oud fragrances lean toward one or two of these, though the finest tend to shift between them over the course of a wear.

**Animalic** is the face that divides people. Leather, musk, something raw and almost feral. When people say oud smells like a barnyard or a stable, this is what they mean. On the right skin — and it is very much a skin-dependent quality — animalic oud reads as intensely intimate, like the smell of warm skin rather than anything agricultural. On the wrong skin, or in too high a concentration, it can overwhelm.

**Woody** is the most legible face. The deep, resonant wood of the agarwood itself, minus most of the animalic character. Cedar and sandalwood are approachable cousins; oud at its woody extreme is denser and more complex, with a darkness those lighter woods don't carry.

**Medicinal** is unexpected the first time you encounter it. Camphor, iodine, something almost pharmaceutical. It sounds unappealing until you smell it in context, where it contributes a sharpness and clarity that keeps heavy oud compositions from becoming suffocating. Hindi and Thai ouds tend to carry this quality most prominently.

**Sweet** is the face most commonly found in commercial oud fragrances aimed at Western markets. Honeyed, sometimes figlike, occasionally with a dried fruit character. Cambodian oud leans this way naturally. It's the most immediately accessible expression, and the one most often amplified or approximated by synthetic ingredients.

Learning to identify which face a particular oud is showing you — and how it shifts as it dries down — is one of the more rewarding skills in fragrance.

## Natural oud vs synthetic oud

The commercial reality of oud is this: genuine natural oud is extraordinarily expensive, and most fragrances marketed as oud contain little or none of it.

This is not inherently a problem. Synthetic oud aromachemicals — the most well-known being Agarwood CO2 Extract, Oud Firmenich, and various proprietary accords — have become genuinely sophisticated. A skilled perfumer using synthetic oud can capture aspects of the real material that make a fragrance deeply satisfying, especially to those who find natural oud too confrontational.

What's lost is the complexity. Natural oud has hundreds of aromatic compounds working simultaneously. The animalic, woody, medicinal, and sweet faces appear, recede, and interact over the course of hours. Synthetic oud tends toward one register and stays there. It's consistent in a way natural oud never is, which is both its strength and its limitation.

The practical implication: a fragrance priced at $80 that claims to feature oud is almost certainly using a synthetic accord. A fragrance priced at $400 and upward might contain natural oud, but won't necessarily. Price is not a reliable indicator of naturality in oud — it's an indicator of what the brand thinks the market will bear.

What matters more than natural vs synthetic is whether the oud in the composition — whatever its origin — is doing something interesting. A well-constructed synthetic oud accord can be more compelling than a poorly handled natural one.

## How houses approach oud

The most interesting oud fragrances tend to come from one of several distinct approaches.

Some houses treat oud as a solist — minimal supporting cast, the wood left to express itself with only light accompaniment. These compositions are the most educational: nothing to hide behind, the quality of the oud entirely exposed. They are also the most demanding to wear.

Others build oud as a foundation, laying everything else on top of it. Rose and oud is the classic example — the floral softening the wood, the wood deepening the floral, each transforming the other. Sandalwood and oud, amber and oud, leather and oud follow similar logic.

A third approach uses oud as a finishing note — a depth charge dropped into an otherwise conventional fragrance. The oud here is often synthetic, present in small quantity, providing warmth and longevity without announcing itself. Many popular fragrances work this way without being marketed as oud compositions at all.

And then there are the maximalist approaches: oud stacked on oud, multiple expressions of the material layered until the fragrance becomes something almost architectural in its density. These are the compositions that demand occasion and conviction. They reward both.

## How oud layers with other families

Oud is one of the most layerable materials in perfumery, in part because it has strong affinity with so many different note families.

**Rose** is the classic pairing — so classic it has its own name in Middle Eastern perfumery. The relationship works because rose's floral warmth softens oud's animalic edge, while oud gives rose a gravity and longevity it wouldn't otherwise have. The combination is simultaneously approachable and complex.

**Sandalwood** and oud share structural chemistry. Both are woody resins, but they pull in opposite directions — sandalwood is creamy, soft, meditative; oud is dark, assertive, alive. Layered together they create a woody depth that neither achieves alone.

**Amber** (as a composition rather than a single note — typically labdanum, benzoin, and vanilla) wraps oud in warmth, amplifying its sweet face and suppressing its animalic one. This is the most accessible oud pairing for those new to the material. It also happens to be the foundation of a significant proportion of Middle Eastern fragrance tradition.

**Leather** and oud share the animalic register, which makes their combination either a study in controlled intensity or an exercise in too-much, depending entirely on the construction and the wearer's skin. When it works, it's authoritative. When it doesn't, it's simply aggressive.

What oud layers poorly with, generally, is the fresh and aquatic families. The contrast is too sharp — the lightness of citrus or marine notes struggles to coexist with oud's density. Exceptions exist, but they tend to require considerable skill from the perfumer.

## How to evaluate an oud fragrance before committing

Oud fragrances change more dramatically over time than almost any other category. First impressions are particularly unreliable.

The animalic opening of a Hindi oud can be startling on first spray, softening into something profound over thirty minutes. A Cambodian oud might smell almost generic on first contact, then reveal extraordinary complexity in the drydown. Judging oud in the first ten minutes is like reading the first sentence of a novel and deciding whether to finish it.

The practical approach: apply to skin, leave it alone for at least an hour, and evaluate what it has become rather than what it announced itself as. Pay attention to how it changes — whether it reveals new faces as the top notes fade, whether the animalic quality reads as sensual or confrontational on your particular skin, whether the base feels like it has somewhere interesting to go.

Wear it before you buy it, if at all possible. Oud is one of the note families where skin chemistry most dramatically affects the result. What smells extraordinary on one person can be genuinely challenging on another. This is not a flaw — it is the material expressing itself honestly.
